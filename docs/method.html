Method notes

The detection rule in full, the specification choices, the two places the obvious approach is wrong, and where this is thin.

Detecting the trip

Nothing in the schedule file says "rodeo road trip", and no list of rodeo dates is read in. For each team-season the rule is:

  1. Split the season into maximal runs of consecutive road games.
  2. Keep the runs whose first game falls between 20 January and 20 March.
  3. Take the longest of those. Ties go to the earlier one.
  4. Discard it if it is shorter than four games.

Anchoring on the first game rather than on any overlap matters for the handful of runs that straddle the window edge: a road trip that begins on 14 January and runs into February is a January trip that happens to be long, not a February one, and including it would let the schedule maker's January decisions into a February question.

The rule is applied identically to all thirty-two franchise codes. For San Antonio it returns the rodeo trip in all twenty seasons. For everyone else it returns whatever their schedule happened to hand them, which is the comparison group in output/team_trips.csv and figure 02.

External check. Three of the twenty were checked against contemporaneous reporting rather than against the data that produced them. The detector puts the 2016-17 trip's first game on 6 February 2017, which is the day local coverage described the team as kicking off the fifteenth annual trek; it ends the 2018-19 trip on 25 February 2019, and the league's own write-up of the team regrouping after the rodeo road trip is dated the 26th; and the eight opponents a season-preview piece listed for 2022-23 are eight of the nine games the detector found. It is the right event.

The fifteenth in 2017 also dates the tradition. Counting back puts the first one in February 2003, the season the arena opened, which is why this project starts at 2003-04 and treats the whole sample as having the trip in it.

Is the window doing the work?

I picked 20 January to 20 March before looking at a single result, on the grounds that it brackets the rodeo without being so wide that it starts collecting other teams' western swings. The window, the length floor and the control set are all choices, though, so sensitivity() refits the entire analysis under seven combinations and writes output/sensitivity.csv:

Three things fall out of that table that the README does not stop to say.

The window is not what makes the trip unique. Widening it to the entire January-to-April stretch changes San Antonio's mean trip length by nothing at all — still 8.00 games, still twenty seasons. The rodeo trip is not merely their longest February road run, it is their longest road run of the whole second half of the season, every year for twenty years. No definition finds them a longer one.

Neither number is fragile. The trip coefficient sits between +0.02 and +0.30 under every definition, against standard errors of 0.27 to 0.44. San Antonio's own deviation sits between −0.45 and −0.84, against a 95% interval that is about four points wide in every specification. The nulls are not artifacts of where the window was drawn.

The slate gap is the sensitive one. It ranges from +0.30 to +0.83, and the bottom row is why. The Nth-straight-road-game control carries +0.10 per game, so a model with it in expects more of a team eight games into a trip than one without it, and the trip games sit at a mean depth of 4.6 road games against 1.7 for the rest of the road schedule. Drop that control and the gap halves.

The last row is worth keeping in view for another reason: the sibling schedule project fits the same Nth-road-game term, gets the same slightly positive sign, and says explicitly that it does not believe it. Anything downstream of it inherits that doubt. What survives the doubt is the direction — the trip slate is easier either way — and both nulls.

Two rows miss seasons. Requiring six games drops 2017-18, whose trip was six games long but is caught by the four- and five-game floors — a boundary case, not a finding. The narrow 1 February – 10 March window drops five rodeo trips: the four that started in late January (2005, 2006, 2007 and 2008) and the compressed 2020-21 one, which ran in March. That row's 16 seasons are therefore 15 rodeo trips plus one shorter run the detector picked up instead, which is why its mean length and slate gap are the odd ones out.

The trip enters on one side only

Every other schedule term in this model is differenced — home value minus away value — which imposes that a burden costs whoever carries it the same amount. A road trip cannot be differenced that way, because the home team is never on one. So d_trip is minus the away team's indicator, and the coefficient reads as the effect on the away team's own net rating.

The after-trip term is differenced, because two teams can both be in that state in the same game, and occasionally are. "First games back" is the next N games in date order, whatever venue they are played at — 72% of them are at home, and nothing in the definition requires it — with N matching the length of the trip that preceded it: eight games away, eight games watched afterwards.

That definition matters more than any other choice in this project, and it is the soft spot in the results. output/sensitivity.csv carries the alternatives: a fixed three-game window gives −0.55 (se 0.36), a fixed five-game window −0.19 (se 0.29), and matching the trip's own length −0.13 (se 0.28). Across all nine specifications the term ranges from −0.55 to +0.54. None reaches significance and the spread is wider than any single estimate, which is the honest summary: on twenty trips this data cannot separate a hangover of half a point per 100 from nothing. The README says the hangover is not there; it should be read as saying that nothing here can find one.

Why the win-rate comparison is the wrong one, twice

The folklore compares trip games to other road games. That comparison is wrong in two independent ways, and they are worth separating because only one of them is about the schedule.

The slates are different. The model's fitted value for a game is what it expects of that Spurs team in that situation — its own team-season effect is in there, along with the opponent's, the venue, the rest and the travel. Averaged over the trip games it is +2.46 per 100; over the other road games, +1.84. Since the Spurs term is common to both, the 0.6 between them is entirely opponent and schedule.

About a third of it is the opponents: 50% of trip opponents are eastern-conference teams against 33% of other road opponents, because a two-week block has to be assembled out of whoever is at home and a Texas team's eastern swing is the natural shape for one. Most of the rest is the Nth-straight-road-game term, which the trip games sit deep in (mean 4.6 against 1.7) and which carries a small positive coefficient the sibling project explicitly disbelieves. Dropping that control halves the gap to 0.33 and leaves every conclusion where it was.

The margins are identical and the wins are not. San Antonio's actual net rating is +1.74 on the trip and +1.76 elsewhere on the road. The 4.5-point win gap is therefore entirely a close-game record: 58.5% in games decided by five or fewer on the trip, 47.2% off it, on 41 and 178 games respectively. Nothing in this project claims that is skill, and nothing in it could.

The expectation excludes the trip terms. This is the circularity trap and it is easy to fall into. If the fitted value used to define "expected" includes d_trip, then the residual has already had the league-wide trip effect subtracted, and "San Antonio against expectation" silently becomes "San Antonio against other teams on trips" — a different and much less interesting question. So run_02_trips.py drops both trip columns from the fitted value before taking residuals. output/expectation_check.csv reports it both ways: −0.89 circular, −0.71 not. The difference is small and the point is not its size — it is that only one of the two numbers means what the sentence around it says.

What the bootstrap does and does not cover

bootstrap_mean resamples the 160 trip games with replacement, 5,000 times. It prices the sampling variation in those games' outcomes. It does not price the uncertainty in the fitted values themselves, which come from a model estimated on all 23,838 games and are treated here as known.

That understates the interval by a little. It does not matter for what the interval is used for, which is to say the effect is not distinguishable from zero; a slightly wider interval is still not distinguishable from zero.

Where this is thin

No injury or rest data, and this is the Spurs. The team most famous in the league for sitting healthy starters is the team being studied, over the two decades in which they did it. If the rodeo trip was systematically used to rest players — or systematically not, because it was the trip that mattered — that is inside the estimate. The null is "nothing after everything the schedule can see", and a coach's roster decisions are not something the schedule can see.

The All-Star break is inside seventeen of the twenty trips. Seventeen contain an internal gap of five days or more; the model has no term for it. A trip interrupted by a week off is not the same object as one that is not, and with twenty of them there is no way to separate the two.

Twenty trips is twenty observations. The game-level estimates use 160 games, but games inside one trip are not independent of each other, and the effective sample for anything about trips rather than about games on trips is closer to twenty. Figure 04 is drawn with that in mind: it is a display of variation, not a set of twenty estimates.

Possessions are estimated from the box score with FGA + 0.44×FTA − OREB + TOV, averaged across the two teams. Fine at the level of a whole game, not fine below it, and never used below it here.

Regular season only, through 2022-23, which is where the public mirror this reads currently ends.
